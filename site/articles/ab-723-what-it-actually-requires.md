If you shoot listings in California, a rule changed underneath you on
1 January 2026. Most of the coverage since has been either alarming or vague,
and the two failure modes it produces are equally expensive: agents who stop
doing normal photo editing they were always allowed to do, and agents who carry
on with AI imagery and have no idea they now owe a disclosure.

Here is the shape of it, as plainly as we can put it.

## The rule

**AB 723**, approved 10 October 2025, added **§10140.8** to the California
Business and Professions Code. It applies to a broker, a salesperson, **or a
person acting on their behalf** — which is worth pausing on, because that last
clause reaches your photographer, your marketing agency and your video vendor,
not just you.

Where an advertisement or promotional material for the sale of real property
includes a **digitally altered image**, it must:

- carry a **conspicuous statement** that the image has been modified, **and**
- provide **a link to the original, unaltered image** by website, URL, or QR code

Where the advertisement is on a website, the unaltered image itself has to be
there.

Two duties, not one. This is the part most summaries get wrong: a disclaimer on
its own does not satisfy the statute. A sentence saying "some images have been
digitally enhanced" meets the first requirement and simply ignores the second.

## What is explicitly not covered

This is the part worth reading twice, because it is where most of the anxiety
lives and none of it belongs:

> lighting · sharpening · white balance · colour correction · angle ·
> straightening · cropping · exposure

None of these make an image "digitally altered" for the purposes of the statute.
The reasoning is coherent: they do not change the representation of the
property. A brightened kitchen is the same kitchen.

So the ordinary work every listing photographer has always done is untouched.
If your editing does not change what is actually there, you are outside the
rule.

## What is covered

The line the statute draws is about **representation**, not about tooling. If an
image shows a buyer something that was not there — or hides something that was —
it is altered, whether the change came from Photoshop, an AI model, or a very
patient intern.

In practice that means virtual staging, sky replacement, removing a neighbour's
car, adding a fire to a fireplace, and generated imagery of any kind.

## The national rule that was already there

AB 723 is a California statute, and the reaction to it has slightly obscured the
fact that **NAR's Code of Ethics already required this nationally**. Article 12
requires REALTORS® to present a true picture in advertising, and Standard of
Practice 12-5 requires disclosing the status of any altered photograph. That
binds every REALTOR® in every state, independent of any MLS rule or state
statute.

If you are outside California, you are not outside the duty. You are outside the
part that specifies *how* — the link to the original.

## What it costs to get wrong

MLS boards commonly fine non-disclosure of virtual staging in the **$500–$5,000**
range, varying by board. The statutory exposure is a separate question and one
for your broker's counsel.

The larger cost is not the fine. A disclosure failure surfaces when a buyer
feels misled, which is exactly when it is most expensive to be the agent who
listed it.

## Where AI video sits

A video built from photographs of a real property is a generated image under any
sensible reading, and it does carry the duty.

That is not an argument against using it. It is an argument for treating the
disclosure as part of the deliverable rather than something bolted on
afterwards. If your vendor hands you a file and a suggested caption, you have
half of what the statute asks for and all of the liability.

The workable version is: an on-screen mark, and a page carrying the original
photographs, reachable by URL or QR. That page is the artefact §10140.8 asks
you to link to. It is not a courtesy and there is no substitute for it.

## One thing worth checking with your vendor

Ask whether they edit your photographs.

It sounds like a quality question and it is not — it is the one that determines
whether the originals page you are relying on actually contains originals. A
vendor who colour-corrects your stills before generating from them has given
you a page of *their* images, and the link you are publishing no longer points
at the unaltered source.

## The short version

1. Normal photo editing is fine and always was. Do not stop.
2. If an image changes what is actually there, you owe **two** things: a
   conspicuous statement, and a link to the original.
3. A disclaimer alone does not satisfy the rule.
4. NAR Article 12 means the duty to disclose exists nationally regardless.
5. The rule reaches people acting on your behalf, so it is your vendor's
   problem too — but it stays your name on the listing.
